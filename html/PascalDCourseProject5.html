POLY STYRENE X-RAY SPEX CHATS w/Neva Chonin!

Over the past year, X-Ray Spex have grown from a band with a larger than average cult following into one of the brightest hopes on the current rock scene. Their rise to the top, which began in 1977 with their appearance on the legendary Live at the Roxy album, and is still peaking with the release of their own LP, Germ Free Adolescents, comes as no surprise to those who have actually seen Spex in action and felt the sheer power of the group.

Me, I can still remember the first time I saw the live… It was at the Marquee one cold night in the fall of ‘77. I was feeling extremely under the weather due to a slight case of frostbite and malnutrition, and was dead certain that nothing shore of a minor earthquake would be successful in dragging me out of my self-induced stupor.

What I failed to realize was that seeing Poly Styrene on stage is synonomous with being caught in a minor earthquake (or a major one at that). Both experiences shake you up considerable. With her hair in braids and teeth in braces, not only dud she get me up and dancing (no small feat in the steam-bath, sardine tin atmosphere of the Marquee) but gave my spirits a much needed boost as well. Nor was I alone in my enthusiasm, as the audience proved by madly pogoing in the limited space available. The evening climaxes with Sid Vicious momentarily abandoning Nancy to leap onstage for the final chorus of Oh Bonadge, Up Yours! Within seconds the rest of the kids followed suit and the club exploded into uncontrollable chaos. And you wonder why I love this band?

A few months after that memorable show, I sat down with Poly in the kitchen of her home in south London and listened as she traced back through the history of the British scene, from the early days at the Roxy Club-where she and Johnny Rotten were labled “n****** lovers” for dancing to reggae music (wasn’t fashionable yet, y’see), through the so-called Summer of Hate when press inspired violence erupted between the punks and the Teddy Boys. Both Poly and I have had late nights with little sleep and so experience a bit of difficulty in coping with the interview situation; hence hald the time is spent in giggles and nonsensical remarks. The comprehensible parts read thusly...

POLY:Our first gig was at the Roxy in Covent Garden. That place was one of the reasons that we were able to get our act together, because it was the only place in London where you could be seen…When it opened up the basic principle was to give any band the opportunity to play and have people come downt o see them. That’s why it was a great club. But different people took it over–the guy who originally made it what it was got kicked out and the owners started running it. It lost all the atmosphere and just got ‘orrible.

NEVA: Hasn’t that happened to the whole movement, though? Now there’s grown-ups singing with record companies and suddenly going punk overnight.

POLY: I suppose it has changed a bit, but then everything changes, dosen’t it? I don’t like thes bands that record companies put together, but you can suss ‘em out a mile away, anyway.

NEVA: Are you interested in appealing to a wide variety of people?

POLY: If you’ve got something to say you should want to say it to as many people as possible, you shouldn’t worry about who it is, you know. To me it’s music...I mean, “Punk” rock.....I guess it’s good ‘cause you need a word to identify yourself. But even within that I’m still me.

NEVA: Alot of originality has disappeared, it seems. Everyone dresses alike.

POLY: Yeah, we’re trying to break away from all that. I never do what anybody else does, I always do what I want. But it’s inevitable that they’re going to do it up to a point ‘cause they want to identify with something, you see. They’re desperately trying to identify themselves with something so they can think “Ah, that’s it–I’ve found my niche. If i put on this shirt or that, I’ll be accepted by punks. I’ll be a punk.”

All they have to realize is that they can only be accepted by themselves. Self-confidence is the most important thing. To come out of yourself and just do what you like and don’t worry about being accepted by anyone. So up to appoint you can slag them off, but up to another point you cannot, ‘cause you hace to look at it from both sides of the coin. You can’t judge anybody from yourself—-you can’t just say, “Oh, they should be doing this and that and they should be doing what I’m doing.” ‘Cause if they was then they’d be copying you, you see. They don’t have to do that.. If they want to be posers, then that’s alright as long as they know they’re posing and say “Yeah, I’m a poser, so what? Big deal!” As long as they know they are. It’s only when they don’t know that they get into a state of confusion!
