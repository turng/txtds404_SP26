BANNICK, SEVERYNS STAGE 'CLEANUP' TO SILENCE CRITICISM

Everybody knows, of course, that you can't always believe everything that is printed in the newspapers. This is not necessarily the fault of papers. Policeman learned long ago to what extent the newspapers can be "handled." There are "old heads" in every police department who could make an excellent living as press agents, if they weren't engaged in police work.

I don't mean to say that newspapers are "bought." Years ago when police graft was organized along lines far diffrent than those which prevail today, some police reporters used to get a regular "divvy" from the membeers of the headquarters force. But I don't think there is a police reporter today who would be guilty of such a thing. Nowadays other and more scientific means are used to fool the papers and through them the public. The competition for news among the papers is keener than ever. All that is neccesary today to get something across with the papers is an understanding of what constitutes "news". Once you know this, the rest is comparitively easy. "News" can then be manufactured and the papers will eagerly "eat it up."

POLICE "MANUFACTURE" NEWS TO GET FAVORABLE PUBLICITY IN NEWSPAPERS; REPORTERS NOT BOUGHT

The manufactured "news" needn't necessarily be untrue. Nearly always it is absolutely true. But the fact remains that it might not have happened unless the newspapers were eagerly waiting to publish it. Most newspapers -- it must be said in all fairness -- are so eager for news that they will publish it, no matter whom it helps or hurts. The news that helps the department is the news that is eagerly brought to the attention of the reporters for the various papers. Unfavorable news is usually dug up by the reporters themselves except when it is brought to their attention by someone in the department who wants discredit reflected upon someone else.

When I first went into the department I knew none of these things. But I got my schooling fast.

I got my first lesson in how to handle the newspapers soon after I became chief. It was my first knowledge of one of the usual practices of chiefs of police when things began to "get warm."

One day, Hans Damm, who was then inspector of police, came into my office and told me he had a hot tip that a campaign was to be launched in the newspapers against the "wide-open" town."

"Is the town more wide-open than it was a month ago, before I came on the job?" I asked.

HANS DAMM GAVE SEVERYNS HIS FIRST LESSON IN POLICE PUBLICITY:"GET OUT A GENERAL ORDER"

"Well, no, not exactly that," replied Damm, "but it's up to you to do something before she busts in the papers. When you see hell going to bust loose, you ought to act to protect yourself. Get the favorable publicity first, before the others use the papers to take a crack at you. You can't just sit still. Do something, man, do something!"

"What ought I to do?" I asked.

"Get out a good stiff order on a vice cleanup," he said." The boys may not take it seriously, but anyway, the newspaper reporters will, and you will get the headlines first."

"Has it been the policy of former chiefs when things are breaking tough to hide behind a general order?' I inquired.

"Yes," He replied. "That's been the policy in all the years I've been here."

I didn't know just what to get out. In my perplexity I read through the general orders of previous chiefs and found that they, too, had apparently been expected to mumble the same formula about a general cleanup within 60 days of taking office. I hesitated about using the same language the others had used before me, but, really, there was nothing else that could be said.
