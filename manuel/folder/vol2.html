The bull Unigenitus, issued in 1713, condemned propositions associated with Jansenism and became a major source of religious and political conflict in eighteenth-century France. Manuel invokes it to show how disputes over belief could lead to administrative repression and imprisonment within a broader system of control.

“Blackstone” refers to William Blackstone, author of the Commentaries on the Laws of England, which strongly emphasized protection against arbitrary imprisonment. Manuel uses Blackstone as a foil to criticize a French system of confinement that often bypassed open judicial process.

The Château du Taureau, in the bay of Morlaix, was one of several fortresses used to detain prisoners under royal authority. Manuel includes it in order to emphasize the broad geography of state confinement.

The château of Saumur, on the Loire, served at various times as a place of detention. For Manuel, sites like this show that administrative imprisonment extended well beyond Paris itself.

The Château-Trompette in Bordeaux was a royal citadel also used as a prison. Manuel presents it as part of a kingdom-wide network of places where the monarchy could confine people by administrative authority.

Pierre-Encise, near Lyon, functioned as a state prison as well as a fortress. The succession of such place names encourages readers to imagine a map of France covered with sites of arbitrary confinement.

The fort of Bréhon in Languedoc formed part of this dispersed network of strongholds. Manuel’s emphasis is less on each site individually than on the cumulative effect of so many prisons across the kingdom.

Mont-Saint-Michel, famous as a religious site, was also used as a prison for religious and political detainees. Its inclusion shows how places of devotion could also become instruments of confinement.

The château of Ham in Picardy was another fortress used as a place of state detention. Such prisons became powerful symbols of arbitrary authority in the political culture of the late Ancien Régime.

The Îles Sainte-Marguerite, off the Mediterranean coast, contained a fortified prison that held both well-known and obscure detainees. Manuel uses it to show that royal imprisonment extended even to island sites.

Saint-Lazare in Paris combined charitable, corrective, and carceral functions. Manuel includes it in a broader continuum of institutions where care, discipline, and punishment overlapped.

Bicêtre was at once a hospital, poorhouse, and prison. Its mixed functions illustrate how social assistance and coercive confinement were often closely linked in the old regime.

Charenton refers here to the asylum and detention site where people judged insane or socially disruptive could be confined by administrative decision. Manuel suggests that claims of madness could become a convenient excuse for removing inconvenient people from public life.

The Picpus and similar religious congregations could receive women whom their families or authorities wished to hide, discipline, or control. Manuel treats such houses as extensions of the wider system of confinement.

The Salpêtrière in Paris combined hospital, workhouse, and correctional functions, and many women were confined there through lettres de cachet or family denunciation. Manuel uses it as a symbol of moral and administrative coercion.

Sainte-Pélagie functioned as a women’s prison and later became known as a site of political detention. In Manuel’s account, it represents the intersection of gender, morality, and state power.

The Magdalonnettes, or Filles de la Madeleine, housed women accused of moral disorder. Manuel groups them with other institutions to show how allegations of vice could justify punitive isolation.

The convent of Valdonne, like other houses in Manuel’s list, could receive women placed there by their families or by administrative order. He presents this practice as imprisonment disguised as moral reform.

The Refuge in Dijon was one of several provincial institutions where women could be enclosed in the name of protection or correction. Manuel describes such places as religious pits in which people disappeared for years.

The Annonciades and the other female communities listed by Manuel formed part of a wider network of institutions used to confine women across the kingdom. Together they reveal the cooperation of family authority, religious life, and royal power in enforcing a hidden parallel justice.
